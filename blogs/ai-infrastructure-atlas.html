      1Learning path of running local AI

      The core path for running AI privately: make an open-weight model fit, run fast, serve many users, follow the rules, and justify its cost.

      Atlas stageSpecific concepts (atlas nodes)What you'd actually do at the bank
      2 · Inference mechanicsPrefill vs decode, memory-bandwidth bottleneck, KV-cache sizingEstimate how many concurrent users a GPU can serve before buying hardware
      3 · Serving enginesvLLM/SGLang, PagedAttention, continuous batching, chunked prefillStand up the model server and tune it so 200 employees don't wait in a queue
      4 · KV-cache & memoryPrefix caching, cache-aware routingReuse the long shared system prompt and policy text across every request, which cuts latency and cost
      5 · QuantizationNumber formats (FP8/INT4), weight quantization (AWQ/GPTQ), measuring quantization impactFit a 70B model onto the GPUs you have, and prove to the business that quality held
      7 · Distributed inference (tensor parallelism only)Splitting one model across 2–8 GPUs in a serverRun a model bigger than one GPU's memory
      10 · Cluster orchestrationKubernetes GPU Operator, MIG partitioning, queue-based autoscalingShare scarce GPUs fairly across teams; run small models on GPU slices
      11 · AI gateway & observabilityTTFT/ITL SLOs, semantic routing, OpenTelemetry, DCGM GPU telemetrySend simple questions to a small cheap model and hard ones to the big one, and monitor every request for audit and incidents
      12 · Benchmarking & costReproducible benchmarking, latency–throughput curves, cost per tokenShow management that running privately costs X versus a cloud API at the same quality, which decides whether the project lives
      Stage 11 matters most for a moat. The gateway is where enterprise rules live: authentication, PII redaction, audit logs, per-department quotas and routing. That's exactly the "encode regulation into controls" work no US platform does for a Vietnamese bank.

      4A DeepLearning.AI route that covers 9 of the 12 stages

      Sixteen courses in five phases, picked from all 132 in the DeepLearning.AI catalog and mapped to the same stages as tables 1 and 2. Each card shows how solid the mapping is: outline means a lesson title in the course outline names the subject; summary means only the course's “What you'll learn” does.

      Where DeepLearning.AI stops. No course in the catalog teaches stage 1 (CUDA kernels), stage 9 (multi-node & InfiniBand) or stage 10 (cluster orchestration), and stage 7 appears only as one optional training-side video. Fill those from the Stanford route above: CS 149 for kernels, CS 111 and CS 40 for orchestration, CS 144 and CS 244C for networking.

      5Coverage map: Stanford, DeepLearning.AI and Fanout.sh

      Each atlas stage from tables 1 and 2 against the three sources. Stanford gives the theory, DeepLearning.AI gives short hands-on courses, and Fanout.sh Inference Engineering is a single 226-lesson track built around measuring and operating real serving engines. A stage is covered when the source names every concept in its table row, and partly when some are missing.

      Taken together, the three cover almost every concept. Each source fills another's gap: Fanout carries the production and economics detail Stanford leaves to papers; DeepLearning.AI and Stanford's security courses carry the guardrails, PII and governance work Fanout skips; Stanford is the only source for InfiniBand fabrics and CUDA C++. Stage 10's MIG partitioning is the one concept no source names: use the NVIDIA GPU Operator and MIG documentation.

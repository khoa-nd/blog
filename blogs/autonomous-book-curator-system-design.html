Alexandria: An Autonomous Personalized Book Curator That Learns My Taste - P1 System Design

Named after the Library of Alexandria, which set out to collect every book in the known world. This one collects only the books worth my time: one a day, picked against my taste, checked against what readers and critics say, and tuned by how I react.

Banner: The Great Library of Alexandria, engraving by O. Von Corven, 19th century. Public domain; scan via Wikimedia Commons (CC0), toned sepia.
The big picture

A loop, not an agent

Seven parts in a fixed order, run on a schedule. The LLM works inside a part with a narrow job and a typed output; it never decides what runs next.

Status: P1 system design, for review. No code until the open decisions in section 12 are settled.
Cadence 1 book / day, 07:00 ICT Stages Discover → Shortlist → Review → Recommend → React Data polite web crawl, no paid APIs AI any model through one gateway Run cost $0 on free tiers, ≈ $3–8 / month paid

What you could learn

Finishing Alexandria teaches more than the code. These lessons carry over to any AI product you build next.

Turning a wish into something measurable

“I want good books” has to become tastes you can name, one pick a day, and a reaction you can count. Most AI projects stall at this step.

When to use AI, and when not to

Plain rules do most of the work, and the model steps in only where judgment is needed. You learn not to make everything an agent.

Making AI earn your trust

Every claim on the page points to a real review, and nothing changes your profile without your approval. You design for “show me”, not “trust me”.

Learning from a few taps a day

Four buttons are enough to steer the system. You see the balance between serving what you already like and trying something new.

Building to a budget

Free tiers, a daily spending cap and backup models. Cost becomes a choice you make up front, not a surprise bill.

Being a good guest on the web

Reading each site’s rules, going slowly, and leaving alone the sites that say no. This is where “can I collect this” meets “should I”.

Life after launch

Something that runs while you sleep still needs looking after: a glance each week, a review each month, and knowing what “broken” looks like.

Something about your own taste

The weekly reflection and the record of your reactions show patterns in your reading you may not have noticed.

1. What Alexandria is, and what it isn't

"Autonomous" here means it runs every day without me: it wakes on a schedule, does the same five stages in the same order, and stops. It is not an open-ended agent that decides its own next step. That choice makes it cheap, testable, and easy to replay when a pick goes wrong.

F2 DiscoverCrawl the bookstore and publisher sites I track. I add sites from the same web form; a seed list comes preloaded.
F3 ShortlistFilter and score candidates against my taste; plan which taste gets today's slot.

Taste is data with a timeline. Every change is an event, so Alexandria can answer "what did I like in March?" and the learning loop can tell a real shift in taste from a bad week.

Register a new taste

A web form on this site first: a console page lists the current facets and lets me add, reweight or pause one. Only requests carrying my owner key count. A free-text box ("more books like The Code Breaker") is parsed into a proposed change that I confirm with one click. Telegram commands come later.

Kept time by time

taste_event is append-only (add, reweight, pause, remove, note). The current profile is a view over it. A monthly snapshot gives a taste timeline I can read later.

Cold start

Seed from the 8 books already on my reading list (titles, ratings, notes), a short form (10 favorites, 5 books I didn't enjoy), and a Goodreads CSV export if I have one. Day 1 picks use these priors.

3. Stage 1: Discover

Alexandria crawls the sites I track: bookstores and publishers I register in a web form, the same way I register tastes. A wide crawl every Sunday builds the candidate pool; a small daily crawl picks up new releases. No paid data APIs.

The form takes a site URL, its kind (bookstore, publisher, prize, review), the pages to watch (new releases, a category), and the language. Before the first crawl, Alexandria reads the site's robots.txt and sets its mode: crawl, or link-only (no fetching; store search links only). The seed list below is preloaded, with modes from robots.txt checked on Nov 2, 2026.

Read the rules first

robots.txt, crawl-delay, and the site's terms decide the mode. A site that forbids robots stays link-only, however useful it would be.

Find pages

Start from sitemaps and the watched listing pages. Never crawl search result pages, and never log in.

Extract

Read schema.org Book / Product data embedded in the page when it's there. Otherwise a small model behind the AI gateway extracts title, author, ISBN, price and rating into a typed record.

Be gentle

At least 10 seconds between requests to one site, a daily page cap per site, conditional requests (ETag) so unchanged pages cost nothing, and a user agent that names Alexandria and links back here.

Everything is stored per work (one book, many editions), keyed by ISBN-13 or normalized title and author, so the same book found on five sites is one candidate.

Design rule. Each tracked site has a mode, a rate limit, a cache and a health flag. When a site starts blocking or changes its terms, it drops to link-only and Alexandria keeps going; it never works around a block.

4. Stage 2: Shortlist by taste

From a few hundred candidates to five, in three passes: hard filters, a cheap score, then an LLM judge on the top twenty.

Pass 1

Hard filters

  • Not already read, recommended or rejected
  • Author cooldown: 30 days
  • Buyable in Vietnam, or a Vietnamese edition exists
  • Enough ratings to judge, except for releases under 3 months old
Pass 2

Cheap score

score = taste_match + quality + freshness + novelty − penalties, each weighted. Taste match is embedding similarity between the book and each facet. Quality is a Bayesian average of the ratings found on tracked pages, so a 4.6 from 12 readers doesn't beat a 4.3 from 40,000, plus a prize bonus.

Pass 3

LLM judge

The judge model, called through the AI gateway, reads my profile and the top 20 book cards and scores each against a fixed rubric (fit per facet, depth, readability, why now), with a one-line reason. Output is typed JSON, so the reasons are stored and auditable.

Which taste gets today? A slot planner picks the facet before the book. It uses Thompson sampling: each facet keeps a Beta distribution of "how often I liked its picks", seeded from my priorities, and the planner samples from each and takes the highest. Math and physics history start ahead, but a facet that keeps getting "Not for me" loses slots on its own. One day a week is a labeled wildcard outside my top facets, so the loop doesn't shrink my taste to what it already knows.

5. Stage 3: Review check

The top three of the shortlist get a full review check. The best of the three after reviews becomes today's pick; the other two are kept as runner-ups.

Gather evidence

  • Ratings and review counts on the tracked store and publisher pages
  • Critics' reviews on tracked review sites, such as The Guardian
  • Optional: the model's own web search, when the provider chosen in the gateway offers it, with every result cited

Each piece is stored with its URL and fetch time.

Write the digest

The digest model writes a structured summary: consensus, strengths, common criticisms, who it's for, how much math it assumes, and red flags (dated science, disputed claims). Every sentence carries the ID of the evidence it came from.

Check the digest

A validator drops any sentence whose evidence ID doesn't exist or whose source text doesn't support it. Ratings are copied from evidence, never written by the model. If too little survives, the book goes back to the pool and the runner-up moves up.

The comparison of the three books is stored too: which one won and why. That gives a ranked group every day, the same group-relative signal GRPO learns from in the Wikipedia agent tutorial.

6. Stage 4: Recommend, one book a day

At 07:00 ICT the pick is published as a page on this site. For the proof of concept that page is the only channel, and it is also where I react. Telegram comes later.

How the page works

  • One page a day in a new "Book suggestions" list on this site, with a short introduction, the review digest with sources, and links to find the book.
  • Reactions on the page: three buttons and a comment box. A small edge function stores the click. Only requests carrying my owner key count, saved once in my browser; visitors' clicks are kept apart and never train anything.
  • Later: a Telegram bot with the same buttons, sending the same stored recommendation.

Publishing. The page is built overnight and goes public at 07:00. From the console page I can hold today's page or skip a day.

Site change needed. One page a day would flood the blog index, so suggestions get their own list (content/book-suggestions.json, rendered on Books), and the blog index shows only the latest three.

7. Stage 5: Recognize my reaction

Only four signals, all on the daily page. Each becomes a row in reaction, then a number in reward. Rewards can be revised: finishing a book weeks later updates the reward of the day it was recommended.

SignalWhereReward
✅ FinishedButton on that day's page, any time later1.0
👍 Want itButton on the daily page0.6
👎 Not for meButton on the daily page0.0
💬 CommentComment box on the daily pageParsed into sentiment and aspects; moves the reward by up to ±0.2 and can propose a taste change

A comment such as "great story but too equation-heavy for a morning read" is parsed by a small model behind the gateway into structured fields: sentiment, aspects (narrative +, math density −), and a suggested change ("lower math depth for weekday picks"). Suggestions never change the registry by themselves; they wait on the console page for one-click confirmation.

Nothing else is recorded: no purchase tracking, no reading-time guesses, and silence is not treated as a reaction.

8. The learning loop, like RL but sized for one reader

One reader produces about 365 labels a year. Full RL on an LLM would starve on that. So Alexandria climbs in levels, and each level only switches on when there is enough data to beat the one below it on the offline eval.

Level 0 · day 1

Priors and rules

Starting priorities, cold-start seed books, the judge's rubric. Works with zero reactions.

Level 1 · from the first reaction

Slot bandit

Thompson sampling over taste facets, updated the moment a reaction arrives. Decides which kind of book I get.

Level 2 · about 50 reactions

Learned scorer

A small logistic model on book features and embeddings, refit weekly on my rewards, replacing the hand-set weights in pass 2. Decides which book.

Level 3 · about 300 reactions, optional

Post-trained judge

SFT on accepted picks plus DPO or GRPO on my preferences, trained on Tinker like the Wikipedia agent. Only if the Level 2 eval shows the off-the-shelf judge is the bottleneck.

Weekly reflection. Every Sunday, the reflection model reads the week's reactions and comments and proposes a profile diff in plain words ("you liked narrative physics history more than technical treatments; lower math depth on weekdays"). I approve or edit it on the console page, and it becomes a new profile_version.

Wikipedia agent tutorialSame idea in Alexandria
Eval first: lock a test setOffline replay: books I've already rated, held out. Metric: how many I rated ≥ 4★ land in the shortlist's top 5. Online: 30-day Want-it rate and finish rate
Harness: the same loop for eval and trainingThe five stages are one pipeline; replaying a past day runs the same code

The AI gateway

Every AI job (extract, judge, digest, write, parse a comment, reflect) goes through one function: gateway.run(task, input, schema). It returns typed output plus the model used, tokens, cost and latency. Which model serves which task lives in one config file, so switching providers is an edit, not a code change.

Adapters

Anthropic, OpenAI, Google Gemini, DeepSeek and OpenRouter. DeepSeek and OpenRouter speak the OpenAI-compatible API, so they share one adapter; OpenRouter also reaches the free models. A local model can be added later.

Same shape every time

Each task has a JSON schema. The gateway validates the answer, retries once with the error, and otherwise moves to the next model in the fallback list.

Fallback and budget

Rate limits, server errors, refusals and timeouts fall through to the next model. When the daily budget is spent, only free routes run.

Compare on my data

Shadow mode runs a second model on a share of a task and stores both answers. The replay eval scores both against my past reactions, so choosing a model is a measured decision. Every call is logged in llm_call.

10. Guardrails

What it may do alone

  • Crawl tracked sites in crawl mode
  • Score and store
  • Publish the daily page

What needs me

  • Any change to the taste registry
  • Adding or removing a tracked site
  • Raising the spend cap

Hard limits

  • Daily caps on AI spend and pages crawled per site; the run stops cleanly when hit
  • One recommendation per date (unique key), so a rerun never double-posts
  • Pause, Skip today and Why this book on the console page

Honest content

  • No claim without evidence; ratings copied, never generated
  • Prices show their check time
  • Pages in my own words, at most one short quote

Polite crawler

  • robots.txt and crawl-delay obeyed
  • Names itself and links back here
  • Link-only when terms forbid robots

Private by default

  • Reactions and taste history stay in the database
  • The public page shows the recommendation, not my reaction
  • Only my owner key can change tastes or react

Gemini free tier

Covers Gemini 3.8 Flash, 3.6 Flash, 3.1 Flash-Lite, 2.5 Pro and Gemma 4, among others. Google doesn't publish per-model daily limits in its docs (AI Studio shows them per account), and third-party reports range from about 20 to 1,500 requests a day. Under Google's terms, free-tier prompts can be used to improve its products: fine for public book data, so keep personal notes on paid routes if that matters.

OpenRouter free models

17 free models in its catalog on Nov 2, 2026. Limits: 20 requests a minute and 50 a day, or 1,000 a day after buying at least $10 of credits. The list changes often, so they are a fallback, not the main route.

Quality, roughly

Public leaderboards in October 2026 put Gemini 4 Carbon, GPT-5.6 Sol and Claude Opus 5.5 at the top, with Kimi K3.1 Pro leading open-weight models, and the order shifts between index versions. Judging fit, summarizing reviews and writing a short page are mid-tier work, so a middle-tier model is likely enough; shadow runs will show whether the top tier earns its price.

Does free fit?

Yes for a proof of concept: about 6 heavy and 10 light calls a day. Route heavy jobs to Gemini 3.8 Flash (free) and light jobs to Flash-Lite (free), with OpenRouter free models as the fallback.

12. Open decisions

Decided items are recorded so the design stays honest about what changed. Open items have a recommended default.

  1. Decided Channel. The proof of concept publishes a page on this site and collects my reactions there. Telegram comes later.
  2. Decided Editions. Show both the original and the Vietnamese edition when a translation exists.
  3. Decided Models. Through the AI gateway, compared by shadow runs and the replay eval.
  4. Decided Formats. Any format; no filter on print, ebook or audiobook.
  5. Open Starting route. A (free), B, C or D from section 11? Recommended: A, with B shadowed.
  6. Open Seed sites. Keep the nine seed sites and their modes, or add others now? Recommended: keep, add more from the form later.
  7. Open Where pages live. A separate "Book suggestions" list plus the latest three on the blog index, or every page in the blog index? Recommended: separate list.
  8. Open Publishing. Public at 07:00 with a Hold button on the console, or a private preview I approve first? Recommended: public at 07:00 with Hold.
  9. Open Monthly cap. Recommended: $10 hard cap, which only matters once a paid route is on.
  10. Open Delivery time. Recommended: 07:00 ICT.

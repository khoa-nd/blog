System design · draft v0.1 for review

Shelf: an autonomous book curator that learns my taste

One book a day, picked for me. Shelf finds candidates, shortlists them against my recorded taste, checks what readers and critics say, sends me one recommendation, and treats my reaction as the reward that tunes tomorrow's pick. This page is the design; nothing is built yet.

Status: design for review. No code until the open decisions at the end are settled.
Cadence 1 book / day, 07:00 ICT Stages Discover → Shortlist → Review → Recommend → React Learning bandit now, reward model later Run cost ≈ $8–10 / month Servers none to manage
The big picture

A loop, not an agent

Seven parts in a fixed order, run on a schedule. The LLM works inside a part with a narrow job and a typed output; it never decides what runs next. Click a part to see what goes in, what runs, and what comes out.

        1. What Shelf is, and what it isn't

        "Autonomous" here means it runs every day without me: it wakes on a schedule, does the same five stages in the same order, and stops. It is not an open-ended agent that decides its own next step. That choice makes it cheap, testable, and easy to replay when a pick goes wrong.

        RequirementWhat it means in practice
        F1 Taste registryMy tastes live in a database with their history. I can add, reweight or pause a taste at any time from chat.
        F2 DiscoverPull candidates from book APIs, bestseller lists, prize lists and web search, including stores that ship to Vietnam.
        F3 ShortlistFilter and score candidates against my taste; plan which taste gets today's slot.
        F4 Review checkGather ratings and reviews (Goodreads, Google, Reddit, critics) and write an honest digest with sources.
        F5 RecommendOne book a day by chat message, email, and an auto-published post in a "Book suggestions" section of this site.
        F6 ReactionsCapture likes, dislikes, comments, purchases, finishes, and silence.
        F7 LearnTurn reactions into rewards that change future picks, the way RL does, at a data rate of one label a day.
        N1 TruthfulEvery rating, quote and price comes from a fetched source with a date. No source, no claim.
        N2 Within the rulesOfficial APIs first. No scraping of sites whose terms forbid it.
        N3 Cheap and quietAround $10 a month, no server to babysit, one summary line a day when everything is fine.

        2. The taste registry

        Taste is data with a timeline. Every change is an event, so Shelf can answer "what did I like in March?" and the learning loop can tell a real shift in taste from a bad week.

        FacetStarting priorityNotes the scorer uses
        History and stories of mathematics1.00Narrative over textbook; biographies of mathematicians count
        History and stories of physics1.00Same; includes cosmology and the bomb-era histories
        History of other sciences0.70Biology, chemistry, medicine, computing history
        AI and computer science0.70Both technical and narrative; seeded from my reading list
        Philosophy0.55Philosophy of science gets a bonus from the science facets
        Psychology0.55Seeded by Thinking, Fast and Slow (rated 4★)
        Pulitzer Prize winners0.50A quality signal that boosts any facet, plus its own slot
        Classics0.40Rotates with new releases
        Hot new releases0.40Published in the last 12 months, from bestseller lists and reviews

        Register a new taste

        From chat: /taste add "history of chemistry" high, /taste pause classics, /taste weight ai 0.9. A free-text message like "I want more books like The Code Breaker" is parsed by a small model into a proposed change, and I confirm with one tap.

        Kept time by time

        taste_event is append-only (add, reweight, pause, remove, note). The current profile is a view over it. A monthly snapshot gives a taste timeline I can read later.

        Cold start

        Seed from the 8 books already on my reading list (titles, ratings, notes), a short chat interview (10 favorites, 5 books I didn't enjoy), and a Goodreads CSV export if I have one. Day 1 picks use these priors.

        3. Stage 1: Discover

        A wide crawl every Sunday builds the candidate pool (a few hundred books a week). A small daily pull adds fresh releases and new lists. Everything is stored per work (one book, many editions), keyed by ISBN-13 and Open Library work ID, so the same book from five sources is one candidate.

        Several sources changed their rules in 2025–2026. The table records what was true when I checked on Nov 1, 2026, and how Shelf uses each one.

        Open LibraryOfficial, free; asks clients to identify themselves and stay under its rate limitsCanonical work IDs, subjects, covers for blog posts
        NYT Books APIOfficial, free key for non-commercial use"Hot new release" signal: weekly bestseller lists

        Web searchGoogle's Custom Search JSON API is closed to new customers and shuts down Jan 1, 2027Brave Search API instead (pay per query, small monthly credit)

        Design rule. Each source is an adapter with the same contract: search(query, facet) → [WorkHit], its own rate limit and cache, and a health flag. When a source breaks or changes its terms, Shelf drops it and keeps going; it never falls back to scraping.

        4. Stage 2: Shortlist by taste

        From a few hundred candidates to five, in three passes: hard filters, a cheap score, then an LLM judge on the top twenty.

        Pass 1

        Hard filters

        • Not already read, recommended or rejected
        • Author cooldown: 30 days
        • Buyable in Vietnam, or a Vietnamese edition exists
        • Enough ratings to judge, except for releases under 3 months old
        Pass 2

        Cheap score

        score = taste_match + quality + freshness + novelty − penalties, each weighted. Taste match is embedding similarity between the book and each facet. Quality is a Bayesian average rating, so a 4.6 from 12 readers doesn't beat a 4.3 from 40,000, plus a prize bonus.

        Pass 3

        LLM judge

        Claude Opus 5.5 reads my profile and the top 20 book cards and scores each against a fixed rubric (fit per facet, depth, readability, why now), with a one-line reason. Output is typed JSON, so the reasons are stored and auditable.

        Which taste gets today? A slot planner picks the facet before the book. It uses Thompson sampling: each facet keeps a Beta distribution of "how often I liked its picks", seeded from my priorities, and the planner samples from each and takes the highest. Math and physics history start ahead, but a facet that keeps getting thumbs-down loses slots on its own. One day a week is a labeled wildcard outside my top facets, so the loop doesn't shrink my taste to what it already knows.

        5. Stage 3: Review check

        The top three of the shortlist get a full review check. The best of the three after reviews becomes today's pick; the other two are kept as runner-ups.

        Gather evidence

        • Ratings and counts: Hardcover, Open Library, Google Books, and the Goodreads rating shown in search results
        • Critics: NYT, Guardian, Kirkus, Nature and Science reviews, found by search
        • Readers: Reddit threads (API or search)

        Each piece is stored with its URL and fetch time.

        Write the digest

        Claude Opus 5.5 writes a structured digest: consensus, strengths, common criticisms, who it's for, how much math it assumes, and red flags (dated science, disputed claims). Every sentence carries the ID of the evidence it came from.

        Check the digest

        A validator drops any sentence whose evidence ID doesn't exist or whose source text doesn't support it. Ratings are copied from evidence, never written by the model. If too little survives, the book goes back to the pool and the runner-up moves up.

        The comparison of the three books is stored too: which one won and why. That gives a ranked group every day, the same group-relative signal GRPO learns from in the Wikipedia agent tutorial.

        6. Stage 4: Recommend, one book a day

        At 07:00 ICT the pick goes out on three channels from one stored recommendation, so they never disagree.

        Chat message (mock-up)

        Shelf · 07:00
        The Making of the Atomic Bomb
        Richard Rhodes · 1986 · Pulitzer Prize, General Nonfiction
        Today's slot: history of physics
        Why you: narrative history of physics, your top facet, and a Pulitzer winner.
        Readers say: three-line digest with sources…
        Ratings ★ x.x (n) · Buy: Amazon · Shopee · Tiki · Vietnamese edition: …
        👍 Want it👎 Not for me💬 Comment📖 Reading✅ Finished⏸ Hold post

        Mock-up only: ratings, prices and the digest are placeholders.

        Channels

        • Chat: a Telegram bot that answers only my chat ID. Buttons record reactions; replying to the message records a comment.
        • Email: the same card as HTML, with signed reaction links.
        • Blog post: a short introduction and review digest with sources, published to a new "Book suggestions" section of this site. Shelf commits the post through the GitHub API, and the site's existing build and GitHub Pages deploy it.

        Publishing gate. The post goes live at 12:00 unless I press Hold post. Fully automatic, with a five-hour veto.

        Site change needed. One post a day would flood the blog index, so suggestions get their own list (content/book-suggestions.json, rendered on Books), and the blog index shows only the latest three.

        7. Stage 5: Recognize my reaction

        Every signal becomes a row in reaction, then a number in reward. Rewards can be revised: finishing a book weeks later updates the reward of the day it was recommended.

        SignalWhere it comes fromReward
        Finished, rated 5★ / 4★ / 3★ or lessChat buttons1.0 / 0.8 / 0.3
        Bought (clicked a store link, then confirmed)Link redirect + chat0.7
        Want itChat, email, blog0.6
        No reaction in 72 hoursTimer0.35 (weak)
        Not for meChat, email, blog0.0
        "Already read"ChatNo reward; book marked read, author kept
        Free-text commentReply to the messageParsed into sentiment, aspects and taste suggestions

        A comment such as "great story but too much equation-heavy for a morning read" is parsed by Claude Haiku 5.5 into structured fields: sentiment, aspects (narrative +, math density −), and a suggested change ("lower math depth for weekday picks"). Suggestions never change the registry by themselves; they arrive as a one-tap confirm.

        Reactions on the public blog post count only when they come from me (a signed link in my email). Visitors' likes are stored separately and don't train anything.

        8. The learning loop, like RL but sized for one reader

        One reader produces about 365 labels a year. Full RL on an LLM would starve on that. So Shelf climbs in levels, and each level only switches on when there is enough data to beat the one below it on the offline eval.

        Level 0 · day 1

        Priors and rules

        Starting priorities, cold-start seed books, the judge's rubric. Works with zero reactions.

        Level 1 · from the first reaction

        Slot bandit

        Thompson sampling over taste facets, updated the moment a reaction arrives. Decides which kind of book I get.

        Level 2 · about 50 reactions

        Learned scorer

        A small logistic model on book features and embeddings, refit weekly on my rewards, replacing the hand-set weights in pass 2. Decides which book.

        Level 3 · about 300 reactions, optional

        Post-trained judge

        SFT on accepted picks plus DPO or GRPO on my preferences, trained on Tinker like the Wikipedia agent. Only if the Level 2 eval shows the off-the-shelf judge is the bottleneck.

        Weekly reflection. Every Sunday, Claude Opus 5.5 reads the week's reactions and comments and proposes a profile diff in plain words ("you liked narrative physics history more than technical treatments; lower math depth on weekdays"). I approve or edit it in chat, and it becomes a new profile_version.

        Wikipedia agent tutorialSame idea in Shelf
        Eval first: lock a test setOffline replay: books I've already rated, held out. Metric: how many I rated ≥ 4★ land in the shortlist's top 5. Online: 30-day like rate and finish rate
        Harness: the same loop for eval and trainingThe five stages are one pipeline; replaying a past day runs the same code
        SFT on its own correct answersPicks I liked, with the judge's reasons, become few-shot examples (and training data for Level 3)
        GRPO: compare a group of attemptsEach day's three reviewed books are a group; occasional "this or that?" questions give clean pairwise preferences
        Ablation with confidence intervalsMonthly report: turn off reviews, the bandit, or embeddings on the replay set, with bootstrap intervals

        10. Guardrails

        What it may do alone

        • Fetch, score and store
        • Message me
        • Publish a post after the grace window

        What needs me

        • Any change to the taste registry
        • Adding a new source
        • Spending above the monthly cap

        Hard limits

        • Daily caps on LLM spend and search calls; the run stops cleanly when hit
        • One recommendation per date (unique key), so a rerun never double-posts
        • /pause, /skip, /why in chat

        Honest content

        • No claim without evidence; ratings copied, never generated
        • Prices show their check time
        • Posts in my own words, at most one short quote; covers credited to Open Library

        Private by default

        • Reactions and the taste history stay in the database
        • The blog shows the recommendation, not my reaction
        • The bot answers one chat ID

        Cost

        Claude prices as of Oct 2026: Opus 5.5 $4 / $20 and Haiku 5.5 $0.10 / $0.50 per million input / output tokens; the Batch API halves both. Token counts below are estimates for one day.

        Judge the top 20Opus 5.525k / 6k$0.22
        Review digests for 3 booksOpus 5.527k / 4.5k$0.20
        Write message and postOpus 5.56k / 2.5k$0.07
        Weekly reflection, per dayOpus 5.56k / 0.7k$0.04
        Total≈ $0.55 / day

        That is about $17 a month at standard rates, or about $8 with the overnight work on the Batch API. Search runs about 15 queries a day; at Brave's reported $5 per 1,000 queries that is about $2 a month, which its monthly credit may cover. Supabase, GitHub Actions and Telegram fit in free tiers at this volume. Using Sonnet 5.5 ($2 / $10) for the judge and digests would roughly halve the Claude line.

        Roadmap

        M0 · week 1

        Registry and bot

        Tables, cold-start import from my reading list, Telegram bot with /taste commands.

        Done when: I can add a taste from chat and see it in the database.

        M1 · week 2

        First daily pick

        Discovery from Google Books, Open Library, NYT and prize lists; shortlist; a daily Telegram card without reviews.

        Done when: 7 picks in a row arrive at 07:00.

        M2 · week 3

        Reviews and publishing

        Evidence gathering, digest with validator, email, blog posts with the grace window.

        Done when: a post goes live with every claim linked.

        M3 · week 4

        Reactions and bandit

        Buttons, comments, link redirect, reward table, Thompson sampling for slots.

        Done when: a thumbs-down visibly shifts next week's slots.

        M4 · months 2–3

        Measure and learn

        Offline replay eval, learned scorer, weekly reflection, monthly ablation report.

        Done when: the scorer beats hand weights on replay, with intervals.

        M5 · month 10+, optional

        Post-trained judge

        SFT plus DPO or GRPO on my preferences, on Tinker.

        Done when: it beats Level 2 on replay and live like rate.

        Open decisions for my review

        Each has a recommended default. Nothing gets built until these are settled.

        1. Chat channel. Telegram, Zalo, Slack or Discord? Recommended: Telegram.
        2. Publishing. Five-hour veto window, approve every post first, or fully automatic? Recommended: veto window.
        3. Where posts live. A separate "Book suggestions" list plus the latest three on the blog index, or every post in the blog index? Recommended: separate list.
        4. Editions. English originals, Vietnamese translations, or both? Recommended: show both when a translation exists.
        5. Buying. Plain store links, or apply to Amazon Associates and the Shopee affiliate program for prices? Recommended: plain links first.
        6. Reddit. Apply for Data API approval, or rely on search results? Recommended: apply, and use search until approved.
        7. Models. Opus 5.5 for judging and writing (≈ $8/month with batches), or Sonnet 5.5 (about half)? Recommended: Opus 5.5, then compare on the replay eval.
        8. Monthly cap. Recommended: $20 hard cap.
        9. Formats. Print, ebook, audiobook? This changes which stores and prices matter.
        10. Delivery time. Recommended: 07:00 ICT.

Mechatronics and Sensor Systems Technology Atlas

A 23-stage path mapped from the curriculum of the Vietnamese-German University's Master's in Mechatronics and Sensor Systems Technology, then re-weighted for one goal: hands-on robots that learn, not traditional programmed ones. It adds the AI stages that the 2020 catalogue does not list, and links each stage to free MIT OpenCourseWare, Coursera and edX material for self-study.

      2The smart-robot path through the stages

      The stages that matter most when the goal is a robot that senses, learns and acts. Each row names the concepts (atlas nodes) and something you could actually build.

      Atlas stageSpecific conceptsWhat you would build
      1 · Sensor PrinciplesTransduction, specs and error, calibrationCharacterise the exact sensor you will later feed to a model, and know its noise floor.
      2 · Electronics LabsOp-amp conditioning, ADC sampling, motor driversBuild the analogue front end for an IMU or load cell and a driver for a small motor.
      4 · Programming & DSPPython, C, FFT, digital filtersA filtering pipeline that removes hum and vibration, with its lag measured.
      5 · Advanced Dynamic ControlPID, state space, observers, Kalman filterA speed or position loop with an estimator, tuned in simulation first.
      7 · Simulation & FEMNumerical integration, modelling, validationA simulator of your own robot that you have checked against data.
      8 · Embedded SystemsMicrocontroller, interrupts, RTOS, embedded LinuxA 1 kHz loop on a microcontroller under a Linux board that runs the AI.
      10 · RoboticsKinematics, planning, ROS 2, graspingA robot arm you can command in Cartesian space, in simulation and on hardware.
      13 · Intelligent ControlMPC, nonlinear and learning-based controlA predictive controller with input limits, compared against LQR.
      15 · Image Processing & VisionCameras, geometry, classical trackingA camera-to-action loop that works without any training data.
      16 · System IdentificationLeast squares, step tests, grey-box fittingParameters for your simulator identified from real logs.
      19 · Machine Learning FoundationsSupervised and classical ML on sensor featuresA tiny classifier that recognises motion from an IMU.
      20 · Deep Learning & PerceptionCNNs, detection, transformers, sensor fusionA perception module that outputs object positions in the robot frame.
      21 · Robot LearningRL, imitation learning, VLA modelsAn arm that does a task it was shown 50 times, with a measured success rate.
      22 · Simulation & Sim-to-RealSimulators, domain randomisation, sim-to-realA policy trained in simulation and stress-tested against shifted physics.
      23 · Edge AI DeploymentQuantisation, TinyML, edge GPUs, safe fallbacksA policy that runs within a real latency and power budget, with a safe stop.
      Classical control stays in the path. A learned policy still outputs targets for a PID or MPC loop, runs on a microcontroller at a fixed rate, and depends on well-conditioned sensors. Stages 2, 5, 8 and 16 are the unglamorous reason a learned arm behaves or does not.

      3Stages you can treat as lower priority for this goal

      Real skills, but they belong to other careers more than to learning robots. If you are enrolled at VGU, these modules are still part of the degree; this is advice for self-study.

      StageWhy it is lower priority
      3 · Smart Materials & Sensor TechWorth a skim for tactile and MEMS sensing, but the materials science itself is optional unless you build soft or compliant robots.
      6 · Automotive Sensors & BusesCAN and functional safety matter for vehicles. For a bench robot they are a short topic, not a module.
      9 · AutomationClassical industrial automation. It is the 'traditional robot' side of the field, so skip it for a smart-robot path.
      11 · Fluids & Heat TransferOnly the Elective I alternative. Take it if you want pneumatic soft robots; otherwise robotics replaces it.
      12 · Environmental SensorsProcess technology is niche. Keep the wireless sensor network and remote-sensing ideas.
      14 · Management & ResearchNeeded for the thesis but learned fastest by running a project. 16.842 on OCW is enough as reading.

      7Learning smart robots by building them

      Thirteen labs that climb from a simulated motor on your laptop to a learning robot arm and a capstone. Each has the code, a done when check and the courses that teach the same skill. Tick a lab when it is done; your progress stays in this browser.

      What was run. The Python scripts in Labs 0 to 4, the tiny classifier in Lab 6, the colour tracker's detection function in Lab 7 (on a synthetic image) and the PPO script in Lab 8 were all run on a Mac while this page was written, and their reference numbers come from those runs. Labs 5 and 9 to 12 need hardware or a GPU I did not have: they follow the sensor's register map and the official LeRobot and NVIDIA guides, and say so.       Your laptop: signals & controlno hardware
      Lab 0Model a sensor before you trust it30 min
      1 · Sensor Principles4 · Programming & DSP

      See what resolution, noise and quantisation do to a reading, and why averaging sometimes works and sometimes does not.

      1. Save this script as sensor_model.py. It uses only the Python standard library.
        # sensor_model.py: what does a noisy 12-bit sensor reading really tell you?
# usage: python3 sensor_model.py [noise_volts]      (try 0.004, then 0)
import random, statistics, sys
random.seed(1)
VREF, BITS = 3.3, 12
LSB = VREF / 2**BITS                      # volts per ADC step
SENS, OFFSET = 0.010, 0.500               # sensor: 10 mV per degC, 0.5 V at 0 degC (TMP36-like)

      Lab 2Tune a PID loop on a simulated motor1 h
      5 · Advanced Dynamic Control17 · Digital Control

      Feel what proportional, integral and anti-windup action each do, on a motor with a saturating supply.

      1. Save this script as pid_motor.py. The motor is a first-order lag with a 12 V supply limit.
        # pid_motor.py: tune a PID loop on a simulated DC motor, no hardware needed
def simulate(kp, ki=0.0, kd=0.0, antiwindup=True, dt=0.005, T=3.0, target=100.0):
    tau, gain, umax = 0.15, 12.0, 12.0     # motor: 0.15 s time constant, 12 rad/s per volt, +/-12 V supply
    w, integ, prev_e, out = 0.0, 0.0, target, []
    for _ in range(int(T / dt)):
        e = target - w
        u_raw = kp * e + ki * integ + kd * (e - prev_e) / dt
        u = max(-umax, min(umax, u_raw))               # the supply saturates
        if not antiwindup or u == u_raw:               # clamp the integrator while saturated
            integ += e * dt
        prev_e = e
        w += dt * (-w + gain * u) / tau                # first-order motor model
        out.append(w)
    peak = max(out)
    overshoot = max(0.0, (peak - target) / target * 100)
    settle = next((i * dt for i in range(len(out)) if all(abs(v - target) < 0.02 * target for v in out[i:])), None)
    return overshoot, settle, target - out[-1]

print(f"{'controller':<28}{'overshoot %':>12}{'settle (s)':>12}{'final error':>13}")
for name, kw in [("P only, kp=0.3", dict(kp=0.3)),
                 ("PI, kp=0.3 ki=2", dict(kp=0.3, ki=2)),
                 ("PI, kp=0.3 ki=8", dict(kp=0.3, ki=8)),
                 ("PI, kp=0.3 ki=8, no anti-windup", dict(kp=0.3, ki=8, antiwindup=False)),
                 ("PI, kp=1.5 ki=8", dict(kp=1.5, ki=8))]:
    o, s, err = simulate(**kw)
    print(f"{name:<28}{o:>12.1f}{(f'{s:.2f}' if s is not None else 'never'):>12}{err:>13.2f}")

      Lab 3Fuse a model and a sensor with a Kalman filter1 h
      5 · Advanced Dynamic Control

      Estimate position and velocity from position-only readings, and see velocity recovered without ever being measured.

      1. Save this script as kalman.py.
        # kalman.py: fuse a motion model with noisy position readings
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
dt, n = 0.1, 300
F = np.array([[1, dt], [0, 1]])                      # state = [position, velocity]
H = np.array([[1.0, 0.0]])                           # we only measure position
q, r = 0.3, 0.8                                      # accel noise (m/s^2) and sensor noise (m), std devs
Q = q**2 * np.array([[dt**4 / 4, dt**3 / 2], [dt**3 / 2, dt**2]])
R = np.array([[r**2]])

truth = np.zeros((n, 2))
for k in range(1, n):                                # truth: a body pushed around by random accelerations
    a = rng.normal(0, q)
    truth[k] = F @ truth[k - 1] + np.array([0.5 * a * dt**2, a * dt])
z = truth[:, 0] + rng.normal(0, r, n)                # noisy position measurements

x, P = np.array([z[0], 0.0]), np.eye(2)
est = np.zeros((n, 2))
for k in range(n):
    x, P = F @ x, F @ P @ F.T + Q                    # predict
    S = H @ P @ H.T + R
    K = P @ H.T @ np.linalg.inv(S)                   # Kalman gain
    x = x + (K @ (z[k] - H @ x)).ravel()             # correct
    P = (np.eye(2) - K @ H) @ P
    est[k] = x

rmse = lambda a, b: float(np.sqrt(np.mean((a - b) ** 2)))
print("position RMSE, raw sensor :", round(rmse(z, truth[:, 0]), 3), "m")
print("position RMSE, Kalman     :", round(rmse(est[:, 0], truth[:, 0]), 3), "m")
raw_v = np.gradient(z, dt)
print("velocity RMSE, differenced:", round(rmse(raw_v, truth[:, 1]), 3), "m/s")
print("velocity RMSE, Kalman     :", round(rmse(est[:, 1], truth[:, 1]), 3), "m/s   (never measured directly)")

rng = np.random.default_rng(2)
Ts, N = 0.01, 600
K_true, tau_true = 12.0, 0.15
a_true = np.exp(-Ts / tau_true)

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

def window(kind):
    if kind == 0:   s = 0.02 * rng.standard_normal(win)                                       # still
    elif kind == 1: s = 1.0 * np.sin(2*np.pi*rng.uniform(5, 8)*t) + 0.1 * rng.standard_normal(win)   # shake

    freq = np.fft.rfftfreq(win, 1 / fs)[spec.argmax()]

y = np.repeat(np.arange(3), 300)

    return h, h @ W2 + b2

def find_target(frame):

      Lab 10Teach the arm a task: record, train, run2 days
      21 · Robot Learning20 · Deep Learning & Perception

      Run the full imitation-learning loop on real hardware: teleoperate, record demonstrations, train an ACT policy and let it run on its own.

      1. Check teleoperation first. This should also offer to calibrate any arm that is not calibrated yet.
        lerobot-teleoperate \
    --robot.type=so101_follower \
    --robot.port=/dev/tty.usbmodem58760431541 \
    --robot.id=my_awesome_follower_arm \
    --teleop.type=so101_leader \
    --teleop.port=/dev/tty.usbmodem58760431551 \
    --teleop.id=my_awesome_leader_arm
      2. Log in to the Hugging Face Hub with a write token (the recorded dataset is uploaded there by default) and store your user name.
        hf auth login --token ${HUGGINGFACE_TOKEN} --add-to-git-credential
HF_USER=$(NO_COLOR=1 hf auth whoami | awk -F': *' 'NR==1 {print $2}')
      3. Record demonstrations of one task, such as picking a cube into a bin. The guide suggests at least 50 episodes, 10 per start position, with the camera fixed.
        lerobot-record \
    --robot.type=so101_follower \
    --robot.port=/dev/tty.usbmodem585A0076841 \
    --robot.id=my_awesome_follower_arm \
    --robot.cameras="{ front: {type: opencv, index_or_path: 0, width: 640, height: 480, fps: 30}}" \
    --teleop.type=so101_leader \
    --teleop.port=/dev/tty.usbmodem58760431551 \
    --teleop.id=my_awesome_leader_arm \
    --display_data=true \
    --dataset.repo_id=${HF_USER}/so101_pick \
    --dataset.num_episodes=50 \
    --dataset.single_task="Pick up the cube and put it in the bin"
      4. Replay one episode to test repeatability.
        lerobot-replay \
    --robot.type=so101_follower \
    --robot.port=/dev/tty.usbmodem58760431541 \
    --robot.id=my_awesome_follower_arm \
    --dataset.repo_id=${HF_USER}/so101_pick \
    --dataset.episode=0
      5. Train an ACT policy. The guide says training takes several hours; use policy.device=cuda on an NVIDIA GPU, or a Colab or Hugging Face Jobs GPU, since mps on Apple silicon works but is slow.
        lerobot-train \
  --dataset.repo_id=${HF_USER}/so101_pick \
  --policy.type=act \
  --output_dir=outputs/train/act_so101_pick \
  --job_name=act_so101_pick \
  --policy.device=cuda \
  --policy.repo_id=${HF_USER}/my_policy
      6. Run the trained policy on the robot.
        lerobot-rollout \
  --strategy.type=base \
  --policy.path=${HF_USER}/my_policy \
  --robot.type=so101_follower \
  --robot.port=/dev/tty.usbmodem585A0076841 \
  --robot.cameras="{ front: {type: opencv, index_or_path: 0, width: 640, height: 480, fps: 30}}" \
  --task="Pick up the cube and put it in the bin" \
  --duration=60

      Done when A table of success rate over 20 trials for the policy trained on 25, 50 and 100 demonstrations, and a one-line diagnosis of the failures (grasp, timing, lighting, new cube position).       Lab 11Sim-to-real with Isaac Lab (optional)1 to 2 days
      22 · Simulation & Sim-to-Real21 · Robot Learning

      Train a robot in a GPU simulator with randomised physics, the same method used for legged and arm robots in industry. Needs an NVIDIA GPU, which on a Mac means renting one.

      1. Work through NVIDIA's Getting Started With Isaac Lab path: an introduction to robot learning, then training a first robot with reinforcement learning.
      2. Train the same task twice: once with fixed physics, once with randomised friction and mass.
      3. Evaluate both under shifted physics, and plug in the parameters you identified in Lab 4 as the nominal values.

      Done when A table of success rate under nominal and shifted physics for the two policies, and one paragraph on which randomised parameters mattered. This lab is a plan built on NVIDIA's course; no commands are given because they depend on the Isaac Lab version.

      Lab 12Capstone: a smart pick-and-place robot2 to 4 weeks
      18 · Industry Project & Thesis10 · Robotics20 · Deep Learning & Perception21 · Robot Learning23 · Edge AI Deployment

      Combine everything into one robot, and prove with measurements that the learned part helps.

      1. Define one task and a success rule, for example: place any of three objects into a bin within 30 seconds.
      2. Build a classical baseline: camera detection from Lab 7, inverse kinematics from the robotics stage, and a planner. Measure success over 20 trials.
      3. Build the learned version with the Lab 10 pipeline. Measure the same 20 trials.
      4. Wrap both in safety: joint and speed limits, a watchdog that stops the arm if the policy stalls, and a hardware stop. Log every run.
      5. Publish the repo, the logs, a short video and the comparison table.

      Done when Someone else can rebuild the robot from your repo, and your table shows success rate, cycle time and failure modes for the classical and learned versions side by side. This is the thesis-sized project of the atlas.
